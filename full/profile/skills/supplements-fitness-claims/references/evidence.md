# Evidence standard for supplements and fitness claims

A product claim must be judged by the outcome that matters to the user, not by its marketing, an isolated mechanism, a testimonial, or a single biomarker. Check current sources before making efficacy, safety, interaction, contamination, or regulation claims.

## Evidence hierarchy

Prefer, in order of fit and quality:

1. Current systematic reviews and meta-analyses of the relevant outcome.
2. Well-designed trials in a population and dose matching the question.
3. Consensus statements and official safety sources.
4. Mechanistic studies only as plausibility evidence, never as outcome proof.
5. Testimonials and marketing only as evidence of what is claimed, not that it works.

## Foundational sources to verify when relevant

- Maughan RJ, et al. *IOC consensus statement: dietary supplements and the high-performance athlete* (2018). PMID: 29540367. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29540367/
- Kreider RB, et al. *International Society of Sports Nutrition position stand: safety and efficacy of creatine supplementation in exercise, sport, and medicine* (2017). PMID: 28615996. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28615996/
- Chilibeck PD, et al. *Effects of Creatine Supplementation and Resistance Training on Muscle Strength Gains in Adults <50 Years of Age* (2024). PMID: 39519498. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39519498/
- National Institutes of Health, Office of Dietary Supplements. Dietary Supplements for Exercise and Athletic Performance. https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/ExerciseAndAthleticPerformance-HealthProfessional/

Verify a specific product, formulation, interaction, or jurisdictional claim at answer time with `web_search` and `web_extract`. Prefer current official safety or regulatory sources and relevant research; cite the source, date, scope, and uncertainty. Ask for the user’s country only when regulations or availability matter; assume no national framework.
